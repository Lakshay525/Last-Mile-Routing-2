\begin{abstract}
A last-mile delivery driver visits roughly 150 stops per day. We study the routing of one such driver on the 6,112 real routes of the 2021 Amazon Last Mile Routing Research Challenge. We compare Google OR-Tools with four local-search methods that we implemented: large neighbourhood search (LNS) and simulated annealing (SA), each applied to the whole route (``plain'') and inside a zone-based pipeline (``zonal'') that first orders the delivery zones and then optimises overlapping windows of neighbouring zones. We report estimated travel time, the number of zone changes and the challenge's own similarity score, on 207 held-out routes and with five random seeds. Plain LNS gives the shortest routes, 13.8\% below the drivers' estimated travel time against 10.4\% for OR-Tools (10 s per route), but the routes differ strongly from the drivers' routes. Zonal methods whose zone order is copied from earlier routes of the same depot give routes much closer to the drivers' (official score 0.043 against 0.079 for plain LNS), while still saving 9.7\% of travel time, and need about 0.15 s per route. Simulated annealing matches LNS inside the zonal pipeline but is weaker when used on the whole route. All results are based on estimated travel times from the data set, use no time windows or service times, and come from one data set; we list the limitations in Section~\ref{sec:limits}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Last-mile delivery is the final step of a supply chain: a van leaves a depot with the parcels for one day and a driver brings them to the customers' doors. At the level of one driver, finding the quickest tour is a travelling salesman problem (TSP). Real drivers, however, do not simply follow the shortest tour. They follow habits and local knowledge: they finish one neighbourhood before they start the next, and they avoid some turns and streets. A route planner that ignores this may produce routes that are short on paper but that drivers do not follow \citep{cook2022,ozarik}.

The 2021 Amazon Last Mile Routing Research Challenge \citep{winkenbach2021,merchan2022} released 6,112 real routes together with the order in which the drivers visited the stops, and asked for routes that resemble those of experienced drivers rather than for the shortest tours. The winning team described its approach in \citet{cook2022}: a penalty-based local search for the TSP in which constraints learned from the drivers' routes, above all the order in which drivers visit the delivery \emph{zones}, are added to the objective.

In this report we ask a simpler and narrower question. \emph{Given only the travel-time matrix and the zone label of each stop, how do general-purpose local-search methods compare with OR-Tools, on travel time and on how much the routes look like the drivers' routes, and what does a zone-based decomposition change?} We do not try to reproduce the competition entry and we do not claim a new state of the art.

\subsection{What this report contributes}
\begin{itemize}
  \item An open comparison of five methods under one objective and one protocol: OR-Tools (the baseline), plain LNS, plain SA, zonal LNS and zonal SA. The zonal methods are described in Section~\ref{sec:zonal}. The suggestion to test simulated annealing inside the zone pipeline came from our supervisor.
  \item A zone-change penalty $\lambda$ that lets the same method trade travel time against tidiness, and a study of how it changes the routes (Section~\ref{sec:lambda}).
  \item Ablations of the zonal pipeline (window overlap, neighbourhood size, iteration budget; Section~\ref{sec:ablation}).
  \item A check of our data handling and scoring code against numbers published by \citet{cook2022} on the same 1,107 routes (Section~\ref{sec:lit}).
  \item Five random seeds for every method of ours, and paired comparisons with confidence intervals (Sections~\ref{sec:main} and~\ref{sec:paired}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}
\paragraph{Challenge and winning solution.}
\citet{merchan2022} describe the challenge data; \citet{winkenbach2021} collect the technical reports of the participants. \citet{cook2022} adapt the Lin--Kernighan--Helsgaun (LKH) TSP heuristic \citep{linkernighan1973,helsgaun2000,helsgaun2017} to the problem. They add penalties for visiting zones in an order that earlier drivers did not use, learn this order from ``reference routes'' of the same depot, and add penalties for leaving clusters of zones that share part of their zone ID. Their entry won the challenge (score 0.0248 on Amazon's hidden evaluation set). We use two of their ideas, the zone-change penalty and the reference-route zone order, but with our own, simpler optimisers, and we use their paper as the source of the 1,107-route benchmark of Section~\ref{sec:lit}.

\paragraph{Learning-based approaches.}
\citet{ruizfas} train a graph neural network encoder with a pointer-network decoder per geographical zone on the Los Angeles routes of the challenge. \citet{kool2019} propose an attention model trained with reinforcement learning for TSP and vehicle-routing variants on random instances of up to 100 nodes. \citet{ozarik} frame the challenge as an inverse-optimisation problem: they generate a pool of candidate sequences and select among them with a regression model that predicts the challenge score. \citet{xu2026} study order dispatching and routing for last-mile \emph{pickup} with deep reinforcement learning; this is a different problem from ours, and we include it because it uses graph attention for routing in a last-mile setting. We trained a zone-order graph neural network during this project but it gave no gain over copying a reference route and is not part of the methods compared here.

\paragraph{Local search and OR-Tools.}
Large neighbourhood search \citep{shaw1998,ropke2006} repeatedly removes a set of stops from a solution and reinserts them. Simulated annealing \citep{kirkpatrick1983} accepts a worsening move with a probability that decreases over time; our SA uses the classical 2-opt move \citep{croes1958} and two further moves (Section~\ref{sec:sa}). OR-Tools \citep{ortools} is a widely used open-source solver library; its routing solver is our baseline.

\paragraph{Travel times are asymmetric.} The time from stop $i$ to stop $j$ is generally not the same as from $j$ to $i$ (one-way streets, traffic), so each route is an \emph{asymmetric} TSP. The times are estimates supplied with the data, not measured values.

\paragraph{Zones.} Each stop carries a zone ID such as \texttt{A-2.1E}, set by Amazon's own planning. Drivers tend to finish a zone before they enter the next one, which is why zones matter: a short tour that jumps between zones is often not what a driver would do. There are 8,962 distinct zone IDs across the routes. About 0.7\% of the stops (in 42\% of the routes) have no zone ID; we assign each of them the zone of the nearest stop of the same route (Euclidean distance on latitude and longitude). \citet{cook2022} do the same but look for the nearest stop among the routes of the same depot, so our rule differs slightly.

\paragraph{What we do not use.} The data also contain delivery time windows, package sizes and service times. We do not use them. Our objective is travel time, so our routes are not checked against these constraints (Section~\ref{sec:limits}).

\section{Problem and metrics}\label{sec:problem}
\subsection{The routing problem}
A route has a depot $0$ and $n$ delivery stops $1,\dots,n$ (about 147 on average). $T_{ij}$ is the estimated travel time from $i$ to $j$ in seconds, and $z(i)$ is the zone of stop $i$. A solution is an order $\pi=(\pi_0=0,\pi_1,\dots,\pi_n)$ of all stops, starting at the depot. Following the challenge, the route is an open path: we do not add the way back to the depot. Its travel time is
\begin{equation}
\JT(\pi)=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1} T_{\pi_k\pi_{k+1}} .
\end{equation}
Unless stated otherwise, ``travel time'' means this quantity, and we always report it relative to the driver's own route $\pi^d$ on the same route, $\JT(\pi)/\JT(\pi^d)-1$, in percent. A value of $-10\%$ means 10\% less estimated travel time than the driver.

\subsection{Zone changes and the penalty $\lambda$}
The number of \emph{zone changes} of a route is the number of consecutive stops that belong to different zones,
\begin{equation}
S(\pi)=\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\mathbf 1\!\left[z(\pi_k)\neq z(\pi_{k+1})\right].
\end{equation}
A route that visits each zone in one block has $S=Z-1$ for $Z$ zones; the real drivers average $20.3$ zone changes on our test routes (about 20 zones). A short tour that hops between zones has many more.

The parameter $\lambda\ge 0$ (seconds) is a \emph{price for a zone change}. The methods minimise
\begin{equation}
\Jl(\pi)=\JT(\pi)+\lambda\bigl(S(\pi)+1\bigr)
=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1} C_{\pi_k\pi_{k+1}},\qquad C_{ij}=T_{ij}+\lambda\,\mathbf 1\!\left[z(i)\neq z(j)\right],
\end{equation}
so a route that leaves a zone must save at least $\lambda$ seconds of driving to be preferred. (The $+1$ is the unavoidable first step from the depot and changes nothing.) With $\lambda=0$ the methods look only at travel time; larger values give tidier routes with fewer zone changes, at some cost in travel time. The penalty is only used inside the optimisers: all reported times are real estimated travel times without penalties. We report results for $\lambda=0$ and for $\lambda=60$ s, a value we chose as a moderate setting because it brings the number of zone changes close to the drivers' number (Section~\ref{sec:lambda}); it is a fixed choice, not an optimised one.

\subsection{The official challenge score}
The challenge scores a proposed sequence $B$ against the driver's sequence $A$ by how similar they are; \emph{lower is better and the driver's own sequence scores 0}. The score is the product of two quantities, $\mathrm{SD}\times\mathrm{ERP}_{\mathrm{norm}}$:
\begin{itemize}
\item $\mathrm{SD}$ (sequence deviation) measures how far consecutive stops of $B$ are apart in $A$:
$\mathrm{SD}(A,B)=\frac{2}{n(n-1)}\sum_{k}\bigl(|\mathrm{pos}_A(B_{k+1})-\mathrm{pos}_A(B_k)|-1\bigr)$, where $\mathrm{pos}_A(x)$ is the position of stop $x$ in $A$. It is 0 if $B$ visits the stops in the same order as $A$.
\item $\mathrm{ERP}_{\mathrm{norm}}$ is an edit distance (edit distance with real penalty) between the two sequences, where replacing one stop by another costs their travel time (scaled by the standard deviation of the travel-time matrix), inserting or deleting a stop costs 1000, and the total is divided by the number of edit operations.
\end{itemize}
We re-implemented this score from the public scoring code of the challenge. We checked it against published numbers, but not against Amazon's scorer on the same submissions (Section~\ref{sec:lit}). The score is only a \emph{proxy} for ``looks like what the driver did''. It does not tell us whether a driver would accept the route.

\subsection{Comparing methods}
All methods are run on the same routes. For two methods $a$ and $b$ we compute the difference of their results route by route and report its mean, a 95\% interval (mean $\pm\,1.96$ standard errors over routes) and the share of routes on which $a$ is better. If the interval excludes zero we treat the difference as real. These intervals describe variation between routes only; variation between random seeds is shown separately.

\section{Methods}\label{sec:methods}
Every method gets the same input (the matrix $T$ and the zones) and minimises the same objective $\Jl$. The starting route of LNS and SA is the nearest-neighbour route under $C$.

\subsection{Baseline: OR-Tools}\label{sec:ortools}
We use the OR-Tools routing solver \citep{ortools} (version 9.15) with one vehicle that starts at the depot and ends at an added dummy node whose arcs cost zero. This turns the open path into the closed route the solver expects. Costs are the matrix $C$ multiplied by 10 and rounded to integers because the solver needs integer costs. The first solution is built by the strategy \texttt{PATH\_CHEAPEST\_ARC} (always take the cheapest next arc), and it is then improved by \emph{guided local search} for a fixed wall-clock time: 10 s per route is our main setting (2 s is shown as a fast variant). All other settings are the defaults, and we did not tune them. Because the search is limited by time, its result depends slightly on the load of the machine: two runs at 10 s gave $-10.82\%$ and $-10.38\%$ travel time, and two runs at 2 s differed by 1.5 points. OR-Tools has no training and uses no historical data.

\subsection{Plain LNS}\label{sec:lns}
Large neighbourhood search keeps a current route and, in each iteration, (i) removes a group of $k$ stops (``destroy''), (ii) puts them back at the cheapest positions (``repair''), and (iii) accepts or rejects the new route. Our implementation (compiled with Numba for speed) works as follows.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Destroy.} One of four rules chosen at random with adaptive probabilities: remove $k$ random stops; remove the $k$ stops most related to a random stop (smallest travel time to it); remove the $k$ stops that cause the largest detour; remove stops of one zone. The size $k$ is drawn at random between $\max(2,\lfloor m/40\rfloor)$ and $\max(3,\lfloor m/2\rfloor)$ for $m$ movable stops, so up to \emph{half} of the route can be removed at once. The upper limit was the only LNS setting that mattered on validation routes (a limit of $m/8$ was 1.4 points worse at equal run time).
\item \textbf{Repair.} Either \emph{greedy} insertion (repeatedly insert the stop--position pair with the smallest cost increase) or \emph{regret-2} insertion (insert the stop that would lose most if it did not get its best position).
\item \textbf{Acceptance.} A simulated-annealing rule: accept a better route; accept a worse one with probability $\exp(-\Delta/\tau)$, where the temperature $\tau$ starts at $0.02\,\Jl/n$ and decreases geometrically to $0.01$. The best route found is returned.
\item \textbf{Operator weights.} The probability of each destroy and repair rule is updated from how well it did (the usual adaptive scheme of \citealt{ropke2006}).
\item \textbf{Budget.} 5,000 iterations, which takes about 2 s per route.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Plain SA}\label{sec:sa}
Simulated annealing works on the same route with small moves. Each iteration proposes one move, a 2-opt reversal of a segment (probability 0.4), a relocation of a segment of one to three stops (0.4) or a swap of two stops (0.2), and accepts it if it is better or with probability $\exp(-\Delta/\tau)$ otherwise. The temperature falls geometrically from $\tau_0=0.5\,\Jl/n$ to $0.001$. Because the matrix is asymmetric, reversing a segment changes its cost, and our 2-opt evaluation accounts for it. We use 5,000,000 iterations (about 0.9 s per route); each SA iteration is much cheaper than an LNS iteration. The starting temperature was chosen on validation routes: hotter starts were clearly better than $0.05$.

\subsection{Zonal methods: zone order, then overlapping windows}\label{sec:zonal}
The zonal pipeline (Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}) splits the route into zones and uses the zone structure of the drivers' routes. It has three steps.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{figures/fig_pipeline}
\caption{The zonal pipeline. Windows of $w=3$ neighbouring zones overlap, so a stop close to a zone border can still move into the neighbouring zone's part of the route when the window is re-optimised.}\label{fig:pipeline}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Step 1: zone order.} We choose the order in which the zones are visited. We use two ways to do this.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Order by zone routing.} Treat every zone as one node, with the average travel time between the stops of two zones as the distance, and solve this small routing problem (about 20 nodes) with the LNS of Section~\ref{sec:lns}. This uses no historical data.
\item \emph{Order from past routes.} For a new route we look up the training routes of the same depot and take the one that shares the most zones with it, weighting routes by the driver's quality rating (high: 2, medium: 1.5, low: 1). The zones of the new route are visited in the order in which that past driver first entered them; zones the past route never visited are inserted at the cheapest place. This follows the reference-route idea of \citet{cook2022}, in a simplified form. It needs earlier routes of the same depot, and it copies driver behaviour, so a better official score is partly expected.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Step 2: solve each zone.} In the chosen order, each zone is solved as a small path that starts where the previous zone ended (1,000 iterations of LNS, or 50,000 of SA). The result is a route in which every zone forms one block.

\paragraph{Step 3: overlapping windows.} A window of $w=3$ consecutive zones is re-optimised with the stop before the window and the stop after it kept fixed, then the window slides by one zone. Consecutive windows overlap in two zones, which lets stops near a border move. Inside a window, the optimiser is LNS (1,500 iterations) for \textbf{zonal LNS}, or SA (300,000 iterations) for \textbf{zonal SA}. The objective $\Jl$ is the same as before, so with $\lambda>0$ a stop only moves to another zone if it saves more than $\lambda$ seconds. The zone order fixes the coarse structure of the route; the quality of the zone order therefore limits what the windows can achieve. The window width $w=3$ and the iteration counts were chosen on validation routes.

\section{Experimental setup}\label{sec:setup}
\paragraph{Data splits.} Each route is assigned to the training, validation or test part by a hash of its route ID (60\%, 20\%, 20\%), so the assignment never changes and no route is in two parts. Because the methods are compute-heavy, we work with a random sample of 1,000 of the 6,112 routes (drawn once with a fixed seed). Its test part has \textbf{207 routes} (148 stops and 20 zones on average, 16 depots), and all reported results come from these 207 routes. None of them is in the training part.

\paragraph{What each method uses.} Table~\ref{tab:usage} lists the data each method relies on. Only one component learns from the data: the zone order from past routes, which uses the 3,691 training routes (of the full 6,112) as its library of past routes (a test route is never its own reference). Nothing else is trained. The remaining settings (iteration counts, neighbourhood size, SA temperature, window width) were chosen by trying alternatives on 60 \emph{validation} routes, never on the test routes. The zonal settings were chosen before we fixed the missing zone IDs and were not re-tuned afterwards. The two values of $\lambda$ are a fixed choice.

\paragraph{Seeds.} The random choices of LNS, SA and the zonal methods are controlled by a seed. Every method of ours was run with seeds $0,\dots,4$ (five runs) on the same 207 routes. Run times are taken from a separate clean run: 20 routes, one process, nothing else running, on a Windows PC. Time-limited OR-Tools runs were done with the machine otherwise idle. Absolute times depend on the machine; the ratios matter.

We observe the following.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Plain LNS finds the shortest routes.} At $\lambda=0$ it saves 13.8\% of estimated travel time against the drivers, OR-Tools at 10 s saves 10.4\%, and plain SA 12.3\%. At $\lambda=60$ s the figures are 11.7\%, 9.4\% and 10.4\%.
\item \textbf{Shorter does not mean more like the driver.} Plain LNS has 32.8 zone changes per route at $\lambda=0$ against the drivers' 20.3, and its official score is 0.079; OR-Tools has 35.8 changes and a score of 0.093.
\item \textbf{Zonal methods give tidier routes.} They cut the zone changes to 29 at $\lambda=0$ and to 20.5 at $\lambda=60$, the drivers' level, but they give up travel time compared with plain LNS (11.2\% at $\lambda=0$ and 9.6\% at $\lambda=60$ with the zone-routing order).
\item \textbf{The zone order from past routes has the largest effect on the official score.} With it, zonal LNS reaches 0.043 at $\lambda=0$ and 0.035 at $\lambda=60$ against 0.069 and 0.056 with the zone-routing order, and 0.079 and 0.058 for plain LNS. The price is another 1.5 points of travel time (9.7\% and 8.1\% saved).
\item \textbf{SA and LNS are equal inside the zonal pipeline and differ on the whole route.} Zonal SA and zonal LNS differ by $0.02\pm0.08$ points of travel time (Table~\ref{tab:paired}); plain SA is $1.50\pm0.19$ points worse than plain LNS but $1.92\pm0.24$ points better than OR-Tools.
\item \textbf{The seeds hardly matter.} Over five seeds the standard deviation of the average travel time is at most 0.09 points and that of the official score at most 0.0010, which is far smaller than the differences between methods.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Effect of the zone-change penalty $\lambda$}\label{sec:lambda}
Figure~\ref{fig:lambda} shows plain LNS for $\lambda=0,15,30,60,120$ s. Each increase of $\lambda$ lowers the number of zone changes and the official score and raises the travel time. The curve flattens: going from $\lambda=0$ to $30$ s removes more than two thirds of the excess zone changes (33.0 to 24.3, against the drivers' 20.3) and costs about 0.9 points of travel time; $\lambda=120$ s reaches the drivers' number of zone changes (19.8) with 10.6\% of travel time saved. This is why we use $\lambda=60$ s as the balanced setting. The official score keeps falling with $\lambda$ but stays above 0.054, because matching the zone \emph{count} does not mean visiting the zones in the driver's order.

\subsection{Ablation of the zonal pipeline}\label{sec:ablation}
An \emph{ablation} removes or changes one ingredient of a method and measures how much the result changes; it shows which parts of the method matter. Table~\ref{tab:ablation} and Figure~\ref{fig:ablation} do this for zonal LNS with the past-routes order at $\lambda=60$ s; the last two columns give the change against the default (3-zone windows), by paired comparison over routes.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Overlap helps.} Without overlap (windows of one zone) the saving is $2.62\pm0.25$ points of travel time smaller than with $w=3$; the official score is slightly better without overlap ($-0.0023\pm0.0009$). Wider windows save more time ($w=5$: 9.1\%) and give up a little similarity ($0.0365$ against $0.0349$).
\item \textbf{A large neighbourhood helps.} Allowing only up to $m/8$ removed stops inside the windows reduces the saving by $1.56\pm0.19$ points.
\item \textbf{More iterations do not help.} Between 500 and 4,500 iterations per window, 300 or 1,000 per zone, or two sweeps, the results differ by 0.1 points or less. The zonal method therefore needs only a small budget.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{How large are the savings?}
Estimated travel time is only a part of the working day. On our test routes it averages 10,923 s, while the service time (time at the doors) averages 16,297 s, so travel time is about 40\% of a route's duration. Service time does not depend on the order, so even the best result, plain LNS with 13.8\% less travel time, corresponds to a saving of about 1,470 s, or 5.4\% of the total route duration of 27,220 s. The estimates also ignore parking, construction and real traffic.

\section{Discussion and limitations}\label{sec:limits}
\paragraph{What the results support.} On this data and with these objectives, (i) plain LNS is the best of the tested methods for estimated travel time, and it beats OR-Tools with 10 s per route by a clear margin in under a fifth of the time; (ii) a zone-based decomposition makes routes tidier and much faster to compute, but loses travel time compared with plain LNS; (iii) the largest gain in similarity to the drivers comes from the zone \emph{order}, and that order has to come from earlier routes of the same depot; (iv) simulated annealing is a sound alternative inside the zonal pipeline but not on the whole route.

\paragraph{What the results do not show.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Real driving.} All times are estimates from the data set. We do not know whether a route with less estimated travel time is quicker on the road, and the official score is a proxy for driver acceptance, not a measurement of it.
\item \textbf{Constraints we ignore.} Delivery time windows, parcel sizes and service times are not part of the objective. Routes with less travel time might violate time windows that the drivers' routes respect. We therefore do not claim that these routes are feasible in practice.
\item \textbf{Strength of the baseline.} OR-Tools was run with one configuration (guided local search, 10 s), without tuning, and as one time-limited run. A tuned configuration or a longer limit might narrow the gap. We did not use stronger solvers such as LKH.
\item \textbf{Comparison with the winning solution.} Our best official scores (0.035--0.043) are not better than those of the competition-winning approach, as the check in Section~\ref{sec:lit} shows. This report is a comparison of general-purpose methods and not a competitive entry.
\item \textbf{Data.} One data set, one sample of 1,000 routes of which 207 form the test set, and 16 of 17 depots in that test set. The separate evaluation folder has not been used yet. The past-routes order needs historical routes of the same depot and is not available for a new depot.
\item \textbf{Tuning.} Settings come from 60 validation routes and several were fixed before a data correction (the missing zone IDs); the zonal settings were not re-tuned. The values $\lambda=0$ and $\lambda=60$ are fixed choices. Part of the gap between methods might change with other settings, although the ablation suggests that the iteration budget is not a sensitive choice.
\item \textbf{Statistics.} The intervals in Tables~\ref{tab:paired} and~\ref{tab:ablation} describe variation between routes for one seed, and the seed study uses five seeds. They do not cover other samples of routes or other data sets.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusions}\label{sec:conclusion}
We compared OR-Tools with plain and zonal versions of large neighbourhood search and simulated annealing on 207 held-out routes of the Amazon Last Mile Routing Challenge. Plain LNS gives the largest saving in estimated travel time (13.8\%, against 10.4\% for OR-Tools) but produces routes that differ from the drivers'. A zone-based pipeline with a zone order taken from earlier routes of the same depot produces routes much closer to the drivers' (official score 0.043 against 0.079 at $\lambda=0$) at the price of about four points less travel time saved (9.7\% against 13.8\%), and is about 13 times faster than plain LNS and 67 times faster than OR-Tools per route. The penalty $\lambda$ gives a simple way to move between the two. Simulated annealing and LNS perform equally in the zonal pipeline.

The next steps are the evaluation on the 3,052 routes of the challenge's evaluation folder, and a cost model once cost figures are available.
