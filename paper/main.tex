\begin{abstract}
A last-mile delivery driver visits roughly 150 stops per day. We study the routing of one such driver on the 6,112 real routes of the 2021 Amazon Last Mile Routing Research Challenge. We compare Google OR-Tools, used as the baseline, with three approaches that we implemented. The first is large neighbourhood search (LNS) on the whole route (``plain LNS''). The second is a zone-based pipeline (``zonal LNS'') that first orders the delivery zones and then optimises overlapping windows of neighbouring zones. The third is the same zonal pipeline with simulated annealing (SA) instead of LNS (``zonal SA''). We report estimated travel time, the number of zone changes and the challenge's own similarity score on 207 held-out routes, using five random seeds. Plain LNS gives the shortest routes, 13.8\% below the drivers' estimated travel time against 10.4\% for OR-Tools (10 s per route), but the routes differ strongly from the drivers' routes. Zonal methods whose zone order is copied from earlier routes of the same depot give routes much closer to the drivers' routes (official score 0.043 against 0.079 for plain LNS), still save 9.7\% of travel time, and need about 0.15 s per route for LNS. Inside the zonal pipeline, SA and LNS give nearly the same routes (within 0.22 points of travel time). All results are based on estimated travel times from the data set, use no time windows or service times, and come from one data set. We list the limitations in Section~\ref{sec:limits}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Last-mile delivery is the final step of a supply chain. A van leaves a depot with the parcels for one day and a driver brings them to the customers' doors. At the level of one driver, finding the quickest tour is a travelling salesman problem (TSP). Real drivers, however, do not simply follow the shortest tour. They follow habits and local knowledge, and they finish one neighbourhood before they start the next. A route planner that ignores this may produce routes that are short on paper but that drivers do not follow \citep{cook2022,ozarik}.

The 2021 Amazon Last Mile Routing Research Challenge \citep{winkenbach2021,merchan2022} released 6,112 real routes together with the order in which the drivers visited the stops. It asked for routes that resemble those of experienced drivers rather than for the shortest tours. The winning team described its approach in \citet{cook2022}. It is a penalty-based local search in which constraints learned from the drivers' routes, above all the order in which drivers visit the delivery \emph{zones}, are added to the objective.

In this report we ask a simpler and narrower question. \emph{Given only the travel-time matrix and the zone label of each stop, how do general-purpose local-search methods compare with OR-Tools, on travel time and on how much the routes look like the drivers' routes, and what does a zone-based decomposition change?} We do not try to reproduce the competition entry and we do not claim a new state of the art.

\subsection{What this report contributes}
\begin{itemize}
  \item A comparison of three approaches with an OR-Tools baseline under one objective and one protocol. The approaches are plain LNS, zonal LNS and zonal SA, described in Section~\ref{sec:methods}. The third approach tests simulated annealing inside the zone pipeline.
  \item A zone-change penalty $\lambda$ that lets the same method trade travel time against tidiness, and a study of how it changes the routes (Section~\ref{sec:lambda}).
  \item Ablations of the zonal pipeline: window overlap, neighbourhood size and iteration budget (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}).
  \item Five random seeds for every method of ours, and paired comparisons with confidence intervals (Sections~\ref{sec:main} and~\ref{sec:paired}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}
\paragraph{Challenge and winning solution.}
\citet{merchan2022} describe the challenge data and \citet{winkenbach2021} collect the technical reports of the participants. \citet{cook2022} adapt the Lin--Kernighan--Helsgaun TSP heuristic \citep{linkernighan1973,helsgaun2000,helsgaun2017} to the problem. They add penalties for visiting zones in an order that earlier drivers did not use, learn this order from ``reference routes'' of the same depot, and add penalties for leaving clusters of zones that share part of their zone ID. Their entry won the challenge. We use two of their ideas, the zone-change penalty and the reference-route zone order, but with our own, simpler optimisers.

\paragraph{Learning-based approaches.}
\citet{ruizfas} train a graph neural network encoder with a pointer-network decoder per geographical zone on the Los Angeles routes of the challenge. \citet{kool2019} propose an attention model trained with reinforcement learning for TSP and vehicle-routing variants on random instances of up to 100 nodes. \citet{ozarik} frame the challenge as an inverse-optimisation problem. They generate a pool of candidate sequences and select among them with a regression model that predicts the challenge score. \citet{xu2026} study order dispatching and routing for last-mile \emph{pickup} with deep reinforcement learning, which is a different problem from ours. We include it because it uses graph attention for routing in a last-mile setting. We also trained a zone-order graph neural network during this project, but it gave no gain over copying a reference route and is not part of the methods compared here.

\paragraph{Local search and OR-Tools.}
Large neighbourhood search \citep{shaw1998,ropke2006} repeatedly removes a set of stops from a solution and reinserts them. Simulated annealing \citep{kirkpatrick1983} accepts a worsening move with a probability that decreases over time. Our SA uses the classical 2-opt move \citep{croes1958} and two further moves (Section~\ref{sec:zonal}). OR-Tools \citep{ortools} is a widely used open-source solver library and its routing solver is our baseline.

\section{Amazon routing data}\label{sec:data}

It has 6,112 routes from 17 depots (``stations'') in the United States, each with the stops of one driver's day. For each route the data give the latitude and longitude of the stops, a zone ID for each stop, the estimated travel time in seconds between every pair of stops, package information, and the order in which the real driver visited the stops. Amazon rated each driver route as high, medium or low quality (2,718, 3,292 and 102 routes). Table~\ref{tab:data} gives the numbers we computed and Figure~\ref{fig:data} shows the distributions.

\paragraph{Travel times are asymmetric.} The time from stop $i$ to stop $j$ is generally not the same as from $j$ to $i$ because of one-way streets and traffic, so each route is an \emph{asymmetric} TSP. The times are estimates supplied with the data, not measured values.

\paragraph{Zones.} Each stop carries a zone ID such as \texttt{A-2.1E}, set by Amazon's own planning. Drivers tend to finish a zone before they enter the next one, which is why zones matter. A short tour that jumps between zones is often not what a driver would do. There are 8,962 distinct zone IDs across the routes. About 0.7\% of the stops (in 42\% of the routes) have no zone ID. We assign each of them the zone of the nearest stop of the same route (Euclidean distance on latitude and longitude). \citet{cook2022} do the same but look for the nearest stop among the routes of the same depot, so our rule differs slightly.

\paragraph{What we do not use.} The data also contain delivery time windows, package sizes and service times. We do not use them. Our objective is travel time, so our routes are not checked against these constraints (Section~\ref{sec:limits}).

\section{Problem and metrics}\label{sec:problem}
\subsection{The routing problem}
A route has a depot $0$ and $n$ delivery stops $1,\dots,n$ (about 147 on average). $T_{ij}$ is the estimated travel time from $i$ to $j$ in seconds, and $z(i)$ is the zone of stop $i$. A solution is an order $\pi=(\pi_0=0,\pi_1,\dots,\pi_n)$ of all stops, starting at the depot. Following the challenge, the route is an open path, so we do not add the way back to the depot. Its travel time is
\begin{equation}
\JT(\pi)=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1} T_{\pi_k\pi_{k+1}} .
\end{equation}
Unless stated otherwise, ``travel time'' means this quantity. We always report it relative to the driver's own route $\pi^d$ on the same route, $\JT(\pi)/\JT(\pi^d)-1$, in percent. A value of $-10\%$ means 10\% less estimated travel time than the driver.

\subsection{Zone changes and the penalty $\lambda$}
The number of \emph{zone changes} of a route is the number of consecutive stops that belong to different zones,
\begin{equation}
S(\pi)=\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\mathbf 1\!\left[z(\pi_k)\neq z(\pi_{k+1})\right].
\end{equation}
A route that visits each zone in one block has $S=Z-1$ for $Z$ zones. The real drivers average $20.3$ zone changes on our test routes (about 20 zones). A short tour that hops between zones has many more.

The parameter $\lambda\ge 0$ (seconds) is a \emph{price for a zone change}. The methods minimise
\begin{equation}
\Jl(\pi)=\JT(\pi)+\lambda\bigl(S(\pi)+1\bigr)
=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1} C_{\pi_k\pi_{k+1}},\qquad C_{ij}=T_{ij}+\lambda\,\mathbf 1\!\left[z(i)\neq z(j)\right],
\end{equation}
so a route that leaves a zone must save at least $\lambda$ seconds of driving to be preferred. (The $+1$ is the unavoidable first step from the depot and changes nothing.) With $\lambda=0$ the methods look only at travel time. Larger values give tidier routes with fewer zone changes, at some cost in travel time. The penalty is only used inside the optimisers, and all reported times are real estimated travel times without penalties. We report results for $\lambda=0$ and for $\lambda=60$ s. We chose $\lambda=60$ s as a moderate setting because it brings the number of zone changes close to the drivers' number (Section~\ref{sec:lambda}). It is a fixed choice and not an optimised one.

\subsection{The official challenge score}
The challenge scores a proposed sequence $B$ against the driver's sequence $A$ by how similar they are. \emph{Lower is better and the driver's own sequence scores 0}. The score is the product of two quantities, $\mathrm{SD}\times\mathrm{ERP}_{\mathrm{norm}}$.
\begin{itemize}
\item $\mathrm{SD}$ (sequence deviation) measures how far consecutive stops of $B$ are apart in $A$:
$\mathrm{SD}(A,B)=\frac{2}{n(n-1)}\sum_{k}\bigl(|\mathrm{pos}_A(B_{k+1})-\mathrm{pos}_A(B_k)|-1\bigr)$, where $\mathrm{pos}_A(x)$ is the position of stop $x$ in $A$. It is 0 if $B$ visits the stops in the same order as $A$.
\item $\mathrm{ERP}_{\mathrm{norm}}$ is an edit distance (edit distance with real penalty) between the two sequences. Replacing one stop by another costs their travel time (scaled by the standard deviation of the travel-time matrix), inserting or deleting a stop costs 1000, and the total is divided by the number of edit operations.
\end{itemize}
We re-implemented this score from the public scoring code of the challenge. We checked it on simple cases (the driver's own sequence scores 0, and random orders score about 0.9) but could not compare it with Amazon's own scorer on the same submissions, so small differences are possible. The score is only a \emph{proxy} for ``looks like what the driver did''. It does not tell us whether a driver would accept the route.

\subsection{Comparing methods}
All methods are run on the same routes. For two methods $a$ and $b$ we compute the difference of their results route by route and report its mean, a 95\% interval (mean $\pm\,1.96$ standard errors over routes) and the share of routes on which $a$ is better. If the interval excludes zero we treat the difference as real. These intervals describe variation between routes only. Variation between random seeds is shown separately.

\section{Methods}\label{sec:methods}
We compare a baseline and three approaches. Every method gets the same input (the matrix $T$ and the zones) and minimises the same objective $\Jl$. The starting route of LNS and SA is the nearest-neighbour route under $C$.

\subsection{Baseline: OR-Tools}\label{sec:ortools}
We use the OR-Tools routing solver \citep{ortools} (version 9.15) with one vehicle that starts at the depot and ends at an added dummy node whose arcs cost zero. This turns the open path into the closed route the solver expects. Costs are the matrix $C$ multiplied by 10 and rounded to integers, because the solver needs integer costs. The first solution is built by the strategy \texttt{PATH\_CHEAPEST\_ARC} (always take the cheapest next arc). It is then improved by \emph{guided local search} for a fixed wall-clock time. We use 10 s per route as the main setting and 2 s as a fast variant. All other settings are the defaults and we did not tune them. Because the search is limited by time, its result depends slightly on the load of the machine. Two runs at 10 s gave $-10.82\%$ and $-10.38\%$ travel time, and two runs at 2 s differed by 1.5 points. OR-Tools has no training and uses no historical data.

\subsection{Plain LNS}\label{sec:lns}
Large neighbourhood search keeps a current route and, in each iteration, (i) removes a group of $k$ stops (``destroy''), (ii) puts them back at the cheapest positions (``repair''), and (iii) accepts or rejects the new route. Our implementation (compiled with Numba for speed) works as follows.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Destroy.} One of four rules is chosen at random with adaptive probabilities. The rules remove $k$ random stops, or the $k$ stops most related to a random stop (smallest travel time to it), or the $k$ stops that cause the largest detour, or stops of one zone. The size $k$ is drawn at random between $\max(2,\lfloor m/40\rfloor)$ and $\max(3,\lfloor m/2\rfloor)$ for $m$ movable stops, so up to \emph{half} of the route can be removed at once. This upper limit was the only LNS setting that mattered on validation routes. A limit of $m/8$ was 1.4 points worse at equal run time.
\item \textbf{Repair.} Either \emph{greedy} insertion (repeatedly insert the stop and position with the smallest cost increase) or \emph{regret-2} insertion (insert the stop that would lose most if it did not get its best position).
\item \textbf{Acceptance.} A simulated-annealing rule. A better route is accepted, and a worse one is accepted with probability $\exp(-\Delta/\tau)$. The temperature $\tau$ starts at $0.02\,\Jl/n$ and decreases geometrically to $0.01$. The best route found is returned.
\item \textbf{Operator weights.} The probability of each destroy and repair rule is updated from how well it did (the usual adaptive scheme of \citealt{ropke2006}).
\item \textbf{Budget.} 5,000 iterations, which takes about 2 s per route.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Zonal methods: zone order, then overlapping windows}\label{sec:zonal}
The zonal pipeline (Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}) splits the route into zones and uses the zone structure of the drivers' routes. It has three steps.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{figures/fig_pipeline}
\caption{The zonal pipeline. Windows of $w=3$ neighbouring zones overlap, so a stop close to a zone border can still move into the neighbouring zone's part of the route when the window is re-optimised.}\label{fig:pipeline}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Step 1: zone order.} We choose the order in which the zones are visited. We use two ways to do this.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Order by zone routing.} Treat every zone as one node, with the average travel time between the stops of two zones as the distance, and solve this small routing problem (about 20 nodes) with the LNS of Section~\ref{sec:lns}. This uses no historical data.
\item \emph{Order from past routes.} For a new route we look up the training routes of the same depot and take the one that shares the most zones with it, weighting routes by the driver's quality rating (high 2, medium 1.5, low 1). The zones of the new route are visited in the order in which that past driver first entered them. Zones the past route never visited are inserted at the cheapest place. This follows the reference-route idea of \citet{cook2022} in a simplified form. It needs earlier routes of the same depot, and it copies driver behaviour, so a better official score is partly expected.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Step 2: solve each zone.} In the chosen order, each zone is solved as a small path that starts where the previous zone ended. The result is a route in which every zone forms one block.

\paragraph{Step 3: overlapping windows.} A window of $w=3$ consecutive zones is re-optimised with the stop before the window and the stop after it kept fixed, and then the window slides by one zone. Consecutive windows overlap in two zones, which lets stops near a border move. The objective $\Jl$ is the same as before, so with $\lambda>0$ a stop only moves to another zone if it saves more than $\lambda$ seconds. The zone order fixes the coarse structure of the route, so its quality limits what the windows can achieve. The window width $w=3$ and the iteration counts were chosen on validation routes.

\subsection{Zonal LNS}
The optimiser inside each zone and each window is the LNS of Section~\ref{sec:lns}, with 1,000 iterations per zone and 1,500 iterations per window.

\subsection{Zonal SA}\label{sec:sa}
The optimiser inside each zone and each window is simulated annealing. Each iteration proposes one move. The move is a 2-opt reversal of a segment (probability 0.4), a relocation of a segment of one to three stops (0.4) or a swap of two stops (0.2). It is accepted if it is better, or with probability $\exp(-\Delta/\tau)$ otherwise. The temperature falls geometrically from $\tau_0=0.5\,\Jl/n$ to $0.001$. Because the matrix is asymmetric, reversing a segment changes its cost, and our 2-opt evaluation accounts for it. We use 50,000 iterations per zone and 300,000 per window. Each SA iteration is much cheaper than an LNS iteration. The starting temperature was chosen on validation routes with SA on whole routes, where hotter starts were clearly better than $0.05$, and the same value was used for zonal SA.

\section{Experimental setup}\label{sec:setup}
\paragraph{Data splits.} Each route is assigned to the training, validation or test part by a hash of its route ID (60\%, 20\%, 20\%). The assignment never changes and no route is in two parts. Because the methods are compute-heavy, we work with a random sample of 1,000 of the 6,112 routes (drawn once with a fixed seed). Its test part has \textbf{207 routes} (148 stops and 20 zones on average, 16 depots), and all reported results come from these 207 routes. None of them is in the training part.

\paragraph{What each method uses.} Table~\ref{tab:usage} lists the data each method relies on. Only one component learns from the data. This is the zone order from past routes, which uses the 3,691 training routes (of the full 6,112) as its library of past routes. A test route is never its own reference. Nothing else is trained. The remaining settings (iteration counts, neighbourhood size, SA temperature, window width) were chosen by trying alternatives on 60 \emph{validation} routes, never on the test routes. The zonal settings were chosen before we fixed the missing zone IDs and were not re-tuned afterwards. The two values of $\lambda$ are a fixed choice.

\paragraph{Seeds.} The random choices of LNS, SA and the zonal methods are controlled by a seed. Every method of ours was run with seeds $0,\dots,4$ (five runs) on the same 207 routes. The tables report the mean over seeds of the average over routes, and the standard deviation of that mean over the five seeds. OR-Tools is limited by time rather than by a seed, so it appears as one run (a second run is mentioned in Section~\ref{sec:ortools}). The paired comparisons use seed~0.

\paragraph{Implementation and timing.} Code is written in Python (3.14) with NumPy and Numba (0.68), and OR-Tools is version 9.15. Run times are taken from a separate clean run on 20 routes, in one process with nothing else running, on a Windows PC. Time-limited OR-Tools runs were done with the machine otherwise idle. Absolute times depend on the machine, so the ratios matter more.

We observe the following.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Plain LNS finds the shortest routes.} At $\lambda=0$ it saves 13.8\% of estimated travel time against the drivers and OR-Tools at 10 s saves 10.4\%. At $\lambda=60$ s the figures are 11.7\% and 9.4\%.
\item \textbf{Shorter does not mean more like the driver.} Plain LNS has 32.8 zone changes per route at $\lambda=0$ against the drivers' 20.3, and its official score is 0.079. OR-Tools has 35.8 changes and a score of 0.093.
\item \textbf{Zonal methods give tidier routes.} They cut the zone changes to 29 at $\lambda=0$ and to 20.5 at $\lambda=60$, which is the drivers' level. They give up travel time compared with plain LNS (11.2\% at $\lambda=0$ and 9.6\% at $\lambda=60$ with the zone-routing order).
\item \textbf{The zone order from past routes has the largest effect on the official score.} With it, zonal LNS reaches 0.043 at $\lambda=0$ and 0.035 at $\lambda=60$. With the zone-routing order the scores are 0.069 and 0.056, and for plain LNS they are 0.079 and 0.058. The price is another 1.5 points of travel time (9.7\% and 8.1\% saved).
\item \textbf{Zonal SA and zonal LNS give nearly the same routes.} Their travel times differ by at most 0.22 points and their official scores by at most 0.0013 (Table~\ref{tab:main}). At $\lambda=0$ with the zone-routing order the paired difference is $0.02\pm0.08$ points. At $\lambda=60$ with the past-routes order SA saves a little more travel time ($0.21\pm0.10$ points, Table~\ref{tab:paired}) and needs about five times as long (Section~\ref{sec:time}).
\item \textbf{The seeds hardly matter.} Over five seeds the standard deviation of the average travel time is at most 0.09 points and that of the official score at most 0.0010. This is far smaller than the differences between methods.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Effect of the zone-change penalty $\lambda$}\label{sec:lambda}
Figure~\ref{fig:lambda} shows plain LNS for $\lambda=0,15,30,60,120$ s. Each increase of $\lambda$ lowers the number of zone changes and the official score and raises the travel time. The curve flattens. Going from $\lambda=0$ to $30$ s removes more than two thirds of the excess zone changes (33.0 to 24.3, against the drivers' 20.3) and costs about 0.9 points of travel time. $\lambda=120$ s reaches the drivers' number of zone changes (19.8) with 10.6\% of travel time saved. This is why we use $\lambda=60$ s as the balanced setting. The official score keeps falling with $\lambda$ but stays above 0.054, because matching the zone \emph{count} does not mean visiting the zones in the driver's order.

\subsection{Ablation of the zonal pipeline}\label{sec:ablation}
An \emph{ablation} removes or changes one ingredient of a method and measures how much the result changes. It shows which parts of the method matter. Table~\ref{tab:ablation} and Figure~\ref{fig:ablation} do this for zonal LNS with the past-routes order at $\lambda=60$ s. The last two columns give the change against the default (3-zone windows) by paired comparison over routes.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Overlap helps.} Without overlap (windows of one zone) the saving is $2.62\pm0.25$ points of travel time smaller than with $w=3$. The official score is slightly better without overlap ($-0.0023\pm0.0009$). Wider windows save more time ($w=5$ saves 9.1\%) and give up a little similarity ($0.0365$ against $0.0349$).
\item \textbf{A large neighbourhood helps.} Allowing only up to $m/8$ removed stops inside the windows reduces the saving by $1.56\pm0.19$ points.
\item \textbf{More iterations do not help.} With 500 to 4,500 iterations per window, 300 or 1,000 per zone, or two sweeps, the results differ by 0.1 points or less. The zonal method therefore needs only a small budget.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{How large are the savings?}
Estimated travel time is only a part of the working day. On our test routes it averages 10,923 s, while the service time (time at the doors) averages 16,297 s, so travel time is about 40\% of a route's duration. Service time does not depend on the order. Even the best result, plain LNS with 13.8\% less travel time, corresponds to a saving of about 1,470 s, or 5.4\% of the total route duration of 27,220 s. The estimates also ignore parking, construction and real traffic.

\section{Discussion and limitations}\label{sec:limits}
\paragraph{What the results support.} On this data and with these objectives the following holds.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Plain LNS is the best of the tested methods for estimated travel time. It beats OR-Tools with 10 s per route by a clear margin in under a fifth of the time.
\item A zone-based decomposition makes routes tidier and much faster to compute, but loses travel time compared with plain LNS.
\item The largest gain in similarity to the drivers comes from the zone \emph{order}, and that order has to come from earlier routes of the same depot.
\item Simulated annealing is a sound alternative to LNS inside the zonal pipeline. It gives nearly the same routes and needs more time.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{What the results do not show.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Real driving.} All times are estimates from the data set. We do not know whether a route with less estimated travel time is quicker on the road. The official score is a proxy for driver acceptance and not a measurement of it.
\item \textbf{Constraints we ignore.} Delivery time windows, parcel sizes and service times are not part of the objective. Routes with less travel time might violate time windows that the drivers' routes respect. We therefore do not claim that these routes are feasible in practice.
\item \textbf{Strength of the baseline.} OR-Tools was run with one configuration (guided local search, 10 s), without tuning, and as one time-limited run. A tuned configuration or a longer limit might narrow the gap.
\item \textbf{Not a competition entry.} This report compares general-purpose methods and is not a competition entry.
\item \textbf{Data.} We use one data set and one sample of 1,000 routes, of which 207 form the test set, covering 16 of the 17 depots. The separate evaluation folder has not been used yet. The past-routes order needs historical routes of the same depot and is not available for a new depot.
\item \textbf{Tuning.} Settings come from 60 validation routes and several were fixed before a data correction (the missing zone IDs). The zonal settings were not re-tuned. The values $\lambda=0$ and $\lambda=60$ are fixed choices. Part of the gap between methods might change with other settings, although the ablation suggests that the iteration budget is not a sensitive choice.
\item \textbf{Statistics.} The intervals in Tables~\ref{tab:paired} and~\ref{tab:ablation} describe variation between routes for one seed, and the seed study uses five seeds. They do not cover other samples of routes or other data sets.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusions}\label{sec:conclusion}
We compared OR-Tools with plain LNS, zonal LNS and zonal SA on 207 held-out routes of the Amazon Last Mile Routing Challenge. Plain LNS gives the largest saving in estimated travel time (13.8\%, against 10.4\% for OR-Tools) but produces routes that differ from the drivers'. A zone-based pipeline with a zone order taken from earlier routes of the same depot produces routes much closer to the drivers' (official score 0.043 against 0.079 at $\lambda=0$). It saves about four points less travel time (9.7\% against 13.8\%), and zonal LNS is about 13 times faster than plain LNS and 67 times faster than OR-Tools per route. The penalty $\lambda$ gives a simple way to move between the two kinds of route. Simulated annealing and LNS perform nearly equally in the zonal pipeline.

The next steps are the evaluation on the 3,052 routes of the challenge's evaluation folder, and a cost model once cost figures are available.

To see how close LNS gets to the best possible route, we compared it with the exact solvers of OR-Tools on the first $k$ stops of 15 validation routes for each size $k$ (20 s limit, one worker). Table~\ref{tab:exact} shows the result. CP-SAT (a circuit constraint model) proves optimality for all instances up to 20 stops, but for only 4 of 15 at 40 stops. A textbook mixed-integer formulation (Miller--Tucker--Zemlin constraints, solved with SCIP) is slower at 20 to 30 stops but proves optimality more often at 40 stops (7 of 15). On these small instances LNS found the best known solution in 40 to 67\% of the cases from 15 stops upwards, with a mean excess of at most 0.7\%. On 20 full-size validation routes, CP-SAT (60 s, two workers) warm-started from the LNS route improved it by 0.61\% on average (at most 1.9\%) and proved optimality on 2 of 20. Its proven lower bound is too weak at this size (median gap 24\%) to certify near-optimality. This suggests that plain LNS is within about 1\% of the best possible travel time on a route on average, but it does not prove it.

\section{Reproducing the results}
All numbers and figures of this report are generated from result files by scripts of the project code, which is not publicly released.
